GREENHOUSE

ENERGY

This Garden runs on solar power.

A solar panel charges a battery, which supplies the energy needed to keep the hosting device online. Its availability depends on sunlight, stored charge, weather conditions, and the efficiency of the system.

Every page, image, request, and background process uses energy. This condition shapes how the website is built, hosted, accessed, and maintained. The limited power supply becomes part of the publication itself.

DESIGN RESPONSES

  • lightweight pages
  • reduced and dithered images
  • minimal network requests
  • locally stored assets
  • limited JavaScript
  • reused hardware
  • compatibility with older devices

About the Solar Powered Website

SEED

How might publishing grow under limited conditions?

This Garden begins with a concern about the material conditions of digital publishing. Websites, images, servers, and screens depend on energy, hardware, networks, and continuous maintenance.

These systems are often presented as seamless, while the resources that sustain them remain difficult to see.

This project asks how limited energy, older devices, reduced images, and small-scale infrastructures can shape another approach to digital publishing.

RESEARCH QUESTION

How can computational constraints, material traces, and low-resource infrastructures become editorial and visual strategies for resilient digital publishing?

WORKING PRINCIPLES

  • work within available resources
  • make technical conditions visible
  • reduce without removing meaning
  • reuse and maintain existing devices
  • keep tools understandable and adaptable
  • design for change, interruption, and decay

PERMACOMPUTING

Permacomputing explores how computing can operate within ecological and material limits. It considers energy, hardware, software, labour, and maintenance as connected parts of a technical system.

Its principles encourage care for existing hardware, adaptable tools, renewable resources, open standards, and resilience to interruption. Permacomputing is not a fixed method; each practice responds to its own location, resources, and conditions.

IN THIS GARDEN

  • solar-powered hosting
  • lightweight static pages
  • reused devices
  • limited dependencies
  • visible technical conditions
  • systems that remain understandable and maintainable

These principles guide how This Garden is built, tested, and cared for over time.

PERMAPUBLISHING

Permapublishing applies the principles of permacomputing to publishing. It considers not only the final publication, but also the tools, formats, workflows, and infrastructures involved in producing and distributing it.

Antoine Fauchié proposes three principles: decoupling, deprecation, and empowerment.

These support content that can move between formats, longer use of existing tools and hardware, and publishing systems that people can understand, maintain, and adapt.

IN THIS GARDEN

  • content separated from its presentation
  • materials that can move between web and print
  • simple and reusable file formats
  • lightweight, maintainable tools
  • extended use of existing hardware
  • documented and adaptable workflows

SCREEN MATERIALITY

Screens are not neutral surfaces. Each display is shaped by its own pixel structure, colour range, resolution, refresh behaviour, brightness, and response time. These technical properties influence how images appear, move, fade, and remain visible.

CRT, LCD, OLED, Game Boy, and E-ink screens each produce different visual conditions. Scan lines, limited colour palettes, pixel grids, latency, ghosting, and uneven illumination can be understood as material traces of the display.

IN THIS GARDEN

  • resolution as a visual constraint
  • limited colour and bit depth
  • dithering and visible pixel structures
  • latency and screen refresh
  • E-ink ghosting and image persistence
  • images adapted across different displays

DESCRIPTION

This bed asks what happens when an image is asked to live with less. The same image is passed through a series of reductions. Its dimensions shrink, its tonal range narrows, and its colours are eventually reduced to black and white. Dithering returns some of the missing detail by rearranging the pixels that remain.

The aim is not to find one correct or smallest image. Each reduction is treated as a visual and editorial decision: what remains legible, what disappears, and what new textures begin to grow when less information is available.

EXPERIMENTS IN THIS BED

01 → DOWNSAMPLING

Reducing the number of pixels changes both the size and structure of an image. Details merge, edges become more visible, and individual pixels begin to shape what can be seen.

02 → REDUCED BIT DEPTH

Limiting the number of available tones removes gradual transitions. The image must carry its information through a much smaller visual vocabulary.

03 → DITHERING

Floyd–Steinberg error diffusion and Bayer ordered dithering use different arrangements of black and white pixels to suggest tones that are no longer available.
