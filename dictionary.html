DICTIONARY

A small vocabulary for navigating the garden.

Some words come from computing, some from publishing, and some from gardening. Others have changed meaning a little since arriving here.

GARDEN TERMS

SEED Where the project begins.

A seed can be a question, an observation, a frustration, or something not yet fully understood. This is where the motivations and questions behind The Resilient Garden are planted.

SOIL The ideas the project grows from.

Soil gathers the references, practices, communities, and concepts that support the work.

ENERGY What keeps the garden running.

Digital publishing needs electricity, even when that energy usually stays outside the frame. Energy looks at where the power comes from, how much is available, and what changes when it can no longer be treated as unlimited.

GARDEN BED A place for trying things out.

Garden Beds are practical experiments where tools, materials, screens, code, and publishing methods can be tested. Some experiments grow into larger parts of the project; others remain small tests.

COMPOST Old devices returning to use.

Hardware that has been discarded or left unused does not have to stay that way. Compost is where old devices are repaired, repurposed, or brought back into the garden for a different use.

HARVEST What can be gathered from the experiments.

A harvest is not necessarily a final answer. It is something that has grown enough to leave its original context and become useful elsewhere: a tool, an image, a method, a publication, or a question worth carrying forward.

PRESSED EDITION A piece of the garden that can leave the screen.

The Pressed Edition gathers digital material into a physical form that can be printed, carried, shared, and kept. It is one way for the garden to circulate beyond the infrastructure that originally produced it.

FIELD NOTE A record made along the way.

Field Notes collect observations, technical decisions, failed attempts, references, and small discoveries from the process.

WAYS OF THINKING

PERMACOMPUTING Computing within limits.

Permacomputing asks how computers might be built, used, repaired, and maintained with greater attention to energy, materials, longevity, and existing resources. Instead of assuming that computing should always become faster, larger, and more powerful, it starts from what is already available and asks how much is enough.

PERMAPUBLISHING Publishing that can adapt, last, and remain understandable.

Permapublishing considers the whole life of a publication: the tools used to make it, the infrastructure that keeps it available, and what happens when those systems change or disappear. It favors formats that can be maintained, moved, reused, and read under different conditions.

DIGITAL GARDEN A website that is allowed to keep growing.

Unlike a publication that appears only when everything is finished, a digital garden can hold notes, fragments, references, experiments, and unfinished thoughts. Things can be planted, rearranged, rewritten, or left dormant for a while.

COMMUNITIES OF PRACTICE Knowledge that grows through doing things together.

Communities of practice form around shared activities rather than a fixed institution or discipline. People exchange tools, methods, questions, failures, and experiences, gradually building knowledge that no single person owns completely.

SUFFICIENCY Asking how much is enough.

Sufficiency shifts attention away from maximum performance and toward what is actually needed for something to work well. In this garden, it appears in questions about image size, resolution, energy use, hardware, and how little information a publication might need while still remaining meaningful.

MAINTENANCE The work of keeping something alive.

Maintenance can mean repairing code, replacing hardware, updating a page, reorganizing information, or simply returning to something after some time. It is usually less visible than making something new, but it is part of what allows a system to continue.

WAYS OF SEEING

SCREEN MATERIALITY A screen is not a neutral window.

Every display has its own resolution, pixel structure, refresh behavior, brightness, latency, and way of producing an image. Screen materiality pays attention to these conditions and to the visual traces they leave behind.

GHOSTING A previous image that refuses to disappear completely.

On an e-ink screen, traces of an earlier page can remain after the display changes. Rather than treating this only as an error, the garden looks at ghosting as a temporary record of what the screen has already shown.

DITHERING Making more from less.

Dithering arranges a limited number of pixels or colours so that the eye perceives tones that are not actually there. Here it becomes a way to test how much visual information an image can lose before it becomes something else.

COMPRESSION Making something smaller by deciding what can be removed.

Compression reduces the amount of data needed to store or transmit an image, text, sound, or other file. Sometimes these reductions are invisible; sometimes they leave blocks, blur, noise, or other traces behind.

RESOLUTION How much information an image or screen can hold.

Higher resolution is often treated as automatically better. In the garden, resolution is also a design decision: lowering it can reduce data, expose the structure of an image, and change the way it is read.

LATENCY The time between asking and receiving.

A screen may take a moment to refresh, a server may respond slowly, or a page may arrive piece by piece. Instead of hiding every delay, latency can make the time and infrastructure behind an interface more noticeable.

WAYS OF MAKING

1-BIT Two possible values.

A 1-bit image contains only black and white pixels, with nothing in between. Working with such a small palette makes every pixel count and turns limitation into a visible part of the image.

DOWNSAMPLING Reducing the amount of information in an image.

Downsampling makes an image smaller by using fewer pixels to describe it. Details disappear, edges shift, and new textures can emerge as the image is translated into a smaller grid.

ERROR DIFFUSION Passing an error to the neighbours.

Error diffusion is a dithering method that compares each pixel with the nearest available colour and distributes the difference to surrounding pixels. The resulting patterns can make a very limited palette appear much richer than it actually is

ORDERED DITHERING A pattern for deciding which pixels stay.

Ordered dithering uses a repeating threshold matrix to convert tones into structured pixel patterns. Unlike error diffusion, its underlying grid often remains visible, giving the image a more regular texture.

STATIC HTML A page that does not need much to exist.

Static HTML can be stored as simple files and served without a database or complex application running behind it. This makes a website easier to copy, host, inspect, and keep working over time.

SELF-HOSTING Keeping part of the infrastructure close enough to understand.

Self-hosting means running a website or digital service on infrastructure that you can access and maintain yourself.

THERMAL PRINTING Printing with heat instead of ink.

A thermal printer creates marks by heating coated paper. The process is quick, small, and slightly unstable: images can appear rough, fade with time, and carry the limitations of the printer directly onto the page.
